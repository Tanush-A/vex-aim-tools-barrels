## 1. How does the vendor detect barrels?

### Short answer

**The barrels are found by a pre-trained "AI model" object detector, not by color signatures.** The robot runs three separate pipelines (color blobs, AprilTags, model objects), and `OrangeBarrel`, `BlueBarrel`, `SportsBall` and `Robot` are the four classes of the model pipeline. Each detection is a class id + a box + a confidence score of 1–100, in 320×240 units.

**Confidence: high (about 90–95 %) that it is a learned object detector; essentially certain that it is not the color-signature pipeline.** That it is specifically a *convolutional neural network* is an inference: VEX's AIM pages say "pre-trained AI model" and "AI Classifications" and never say "neural network" or name an architecture. Nothing else ships as a "pre-trained model" that returns per-box class confidences at roughly 10 frames a second on a small embedded camera, so I put the CNN reading at about 85–90 %. **Not public (I looked and found nothing):** the architecture, the network's input size, the training set, the score threshold, the chip.

### What I infer

1. **Barrel detection is the model pipeline, independent of color signatures.** Color signatures are user-configured (hue angle + saturation range, up to 7) and arrive as a different object type; barrels arrive as `aiobj` with a class name and a score, with no signature configured by anyone. (Direct from the API and the library source; near certain.)
2. **The model is a small single-shot CNN detector running on a vision chip with a neural-network accelerator.** Reasons: "pre-trained AI model", "extensive training process", per-box confidence, a fixed class list, about 10 fps on a toy-sized robot, "internal hardware processing with post processing by the CPU" (on the sibling sensor: that is how an accelerator plus box decoding / non-maximum suppression is usually split). Whether the AIM uses the same chip as the V5 sensor is not stated; the near-identical API, resolution and field of view (73° against 74°) suggest the same module. (Likely, not confirmed.)
3. **The detector probably runs on a 320 × 240 (or smaller) image**, since boxes come in those units and the Lab 6 page says it is "actually running on a half-resolution image". The network's true input size is unknown.
4. **The vendor is not illumination-proof.** Dave's lab page calls it "overly sensitive to lighting conditions", VEX's own lesson has students test how light and surface break it, and in the project's data it found 23 of 32, not 32 (and lost to the project on the glass table, 60 against 74 of 75 on train frames).

### What that means for a fair comparison with hand-written color rules

- **It does not gate on absolute color.** A convolutional detector decides from spatial patterns over a window of tens of pixels: the edges of the cylinder, the stripe between two bands of the other color, the contrast with the table, the shadow. Its first-layer filters are mostly oriented-edge and color-opponent filters, which respond to *differences between neighbouring pixels*; a color cast moves all pixels of a neighbourhood together and largely cancels in a difference. On top of that it was trained on images from more than one light (and almost every training recipe adds random hue / saturation / brightness jitter). The project's detector does the opposite: a hard per-pixel test on absolute hue and saturation comes first, and a pixel one hue unit outside the range is gone for every later stage. So the vendor "shrugs off" a cast that moves orange from hue 9 to hue 16, while a fixed sector 3–13 loses the barrel. (Inference; the mechanism is standard, the vendor's training recipe is not public. Note that CNNs are *not* automatically cast-proof — see Afifi & Brown in section 3 — which fits the vendor still missing 9 of 32.)
- **Its typical errors are the typical errors of that family:**
  - *Duplicate boxes.* A single-shot detector proposes many overlapping boxes per object and removes the extras by non-maximum suppression (drop a box that overlaps a better one by more than a set fraction). Two boxes on one barrel that overlap less than that fraction both survive: a partly hidden barrel boxed once as the visible part and once as the whole, or a barrel and its reflection. If suppression is per class, a two-colored object can also get one `OrangeBarrel` and one `BlueBarrel` box. **Cheap check on the saved vendor files: are the duplicates same-class or cross-class, and what is their overlap?** (A first count without the hand boxes: 6 pairs of vendor boxes overlap strongly, 4 of them one orange + one blue on `occl` frames, 2 same-class on `occlx_09` / `occlx_10`. Whether each pair is one barrel boxed twice or two real barrels needs the labels; I did not settle it.)
  - *False boxes.* The score threshold is set for recall on a toy that must "see" barrels in a classroom, so barrel-like patches pass. **Project data (I counted the 155 saved vendor files, 248 objects, all `aiobj`):** 227 objects score 90 or more (median 99), 21 score 63–89, none scores below 63. So the robot seems to cut at about 60 (inference), and the 21 low-score boxes are the first place to look for its false boxes; I did not match them against the hand boxes.
  - *No box for very close barrels.* Up close only the lid shows, the barrel is cut by the image edge and is far larger than usual; that view is likely rare in the training set and outside the box sizes the model was built for. A learned detector answers only for what it was shown.
- **So the comparison is hand rules with two thresholds fit on 16 home frames against a model trained on (presumably) thousands of labelled images across many scenes.** Losing on unseen lights is the expected outcome of that match-up, not evidence that the rules are badly written; winning on false boxes, close-ups and occlusion is the expected strength of explicit geometry.
- **Three things would make the comparison fairer and more informative:**
  1. *Compare at a matched false-box rate.* The vendor's `score` is saved with every frame; sweep a threshold on it and draw found-versus-false curves for both detectors instead of one point each (23 found / 5 false against 14 / 4).
  2. *Report in-condition and new-condition results separately* (already done in the 2026-11-25 report), and say which conditions the vendor has plausibly seen (white surface, bright light — the condition VEX itself calls "controlled").
  3. *Remember the half resolution.* A vendor box edge is quantised to 2 px of the 640 × 480 frame; with the rule "bottom edge within a tenth of the height" a far barrel 20 px tall allows 2 px, i.e. one vendor pixel. Some vendor "misses" at range may be box-accuracy failures rather than detection failures.
- **The transferable lesson is not "learning", it is "contrast and structure before absolute color".** Everything in section 2 that is recommended does by hand what the first layers of that network do: use color *differences* between neighbouring surfaces and the known shape, and let absolute color be estimated late, from objects already found.

## 2. Classical methods for color objects under changing illumination

### Why per-frame HSV threshold adaptation from global statistics was the hard way in

Three separate reasons, each addressed by a family below.

1. **Wrong space.** In hue / saturation a change of light is not one shift: each surface's hue moves by a different amount and saturation changes unevenly. **Project data** (`EXPERIMENTS.md`, 2026-11-16): orange keeps S ≈ 200–215 and drifts 3–5 hue units; blue drops from S 225 to about 100 under the warm lamp and reads hue 26–44 (gray-olive) in the warm bathroom. In log-chromaticity the same change is a single translation shared by every surface (2a).
2. **Wrong statistic.** A global histogram is mostly table and wall, and those change from place to place more than the light does. Any rule of the "average of the scene is gray" kind reads an orange wood table as an orange light (2c).
3. **Color alone has a ceiling.** **Project data** (ceiling check, 2026-11-16): with thresholds set from each frame's own labels the detector found 53 of 152, because under warm light the orange that fits the barrels also paints the wood. No estimator of thresholds can beat that; only structure can (2e, 2h, 2i).

Format of each entry: **Idea** (two sentences) · **Needs** · **Fails when** · **Here** (what it would mean for this detector) · **References**.

### (a) The von Kries / diagonal model, and why a change of light is a translation in log-chromaticity

- **Idea.** To a good approximation a camera channel's response is (how much the surface reflects in that band) × (how much light there is in that band), so changing the light multiplies R, G and B by three gains: a diagonal matrix. Take logs of channel ratios and the multiplication becomes an addition: `log(R/G)` and `log(B/G)` of **every** surface move by the same two numbers, and brightness and shading drop out of the ratio altogether.
  In symbols: `R = E_R · S_R · shading`, so `log(R/G) = log(S_R/S_G) + log(E_R/E_G)`: a surface term plus a light term that is the same for all pixels.
  Finlayson's further step: for black-body-like lights (sun, sky, incandescent, most "warm / cool" lamps) the light terms all lie along one line, so the coordinate at right angles to that line does not depend on the light at all (a one-dimensional "illuminant-invariant" image).
- **Needs.** Channel values roughly proportional to light (a gamma curve only rescales the log axes; a camera's tone curve, color-mixing matrix and JPEG are departures); pixels neither clipped nor near black. For the invariant direction: a calibration, which the project already owns in the form of the same barrels photographed under warm / mid / cool lamps.
- **Fails when.** Real sensors have broad overlapping bands and cameras mix channels for nicer colors, so the shift is only approximately the same for all surfaces; the error is largest for saturated colors, and orange and blue paint are saturated. Clipped channels. Two lights in one frame (lamp plus window) give two shifts. **And the 1-D invariant is the wrong tool for this task:** the direction it throws away is the warm–cool axis, roughly `log(R/B)`, which is the very axis that separates orange from blue from gray. "Orange paint in white light or gray paint in orange light?" cannot be answered per pixel; only other surfaces in the frame can answer it.
- **Here.** Use the *translation*, not the invariant. (1) It explains the measurements: blue's HSV saturation collapsing under the warm lamp while orange's does not is what a shift toward `+log(R/B)` looks like in HSV. (2) It predicts that orange barrels, blue barrels and one given table move **in parallel** between conditions: two numbers per frame instead of six thresholds. (3) The camera's own auto white balance is also a diagonal gain, so it adds to the same translation rather than creating a second problem. **A check that needs no new data:** plot the class medians of the labelled pixels in `(log R/G, log B/G)` per condition and see whether the classes move in parallel.
- **References.**
  - Finlayson, Drew, Funt, "Spectral sharpening: sensor transformations for improved color constancy", *JOSA A* 11(5):1553–1563, 1994 — https://doi.org/10.1364/JOSAA.11.001553; and "Diagonal transforms suffice for color constancy", ICCV 1993 — https://doi.org/10.1109/ICCV.1993.378223. **States** (repository abstract): after a linear change of sensor basis a diagonal scaling models illuminant change very well.
  - Finlayson & Hordley, "Color constancy at a pixel", *JOSA A* 18(2):253–264, 2001 — https://doi.org/10.1364/JOSAA.18.000253. **States** (abstract): "there exists a single invariant color coordinate, a function of R, G, and B" that depends only on reflectance. (Full text not opened.)
  - Finlayson, Hordley, Lu, Drew, "On the removal of shadows from images", *PAMI* 28(1):59–68, 2006 — https://doi.org/10.1109/TPAMI.2006.18 (PDF https://www2.cs.sfu.ca/~mark/ftp/Pami06/pami06.pdf). **States** (read): assumes "perfectly narrow-band sensors" and "Planckian illuminants"; the invariant direction comes from calibration or from entropy minimisation.
  - Finlayson, Drew, Lu, "Intrinsic images by entropy minimization", ECCV 2004 — https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-540-24672-5_46.

### (b) Normalised rgb, opponent colors and ratio invariants: ratios between neighbouring surfaces instead of absolute color

- **Idea.** Pick color coordinates that a given lighting change cannot touch. Normalised rgb and hue cancel brightness and shading but **not** the color of the light; only *ratios between neighbouring pixels* cancel the light's color, because both neighbours receive the same per-channel gain (Funt & Finlayson: differences of the log image; Gevers & Smeulders: `m1 = (R1·G2)/(R2·G1)` across an edge).
- **What the literature states about the project's two features.** van de Sande, Gevers, Snoek (2010), Table I, analytic invariance assuming no clipping:

  | feature | brightness scaling | added offset | change of light color |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Hue | invariant | invariant | **not invariant** |
  | Saturation | **not** | **not** | **not** |
  | normalised r, g | invariant | not | **not** |
  | opponent O1, O2 | not | invariant | **not** |
  | each channel standardised separately ("transformed color") | invariant | invariant | invariant |

  Gevers & Smeulders (1999), abstract: recognition accuracy "degrades substantially for all color features other than m1m2m3 with a change in illumination color."
- **Needs.** Two differently colored surfaces side by side under the same light, neither dark nor clipped.
- **Fails when.** Dark pixels (a ratio of two small numbers is noise). Blur and reduced color resolution mix the two neighbours. A highlight or a shadow edge falls between them. The ratio across an *unknown* boundary (barrel against table) depends on the table, so it identifies nothing; only a ratio between two *known* paints does. (The row "each channel standardised separately" is a white balance of the image by another name, and depends on scene content; it is both forbidden here and fragile.)
- **Here.** The barrel is, by design, two known paints touching: body and stripe. Under the diagonal model the difference `(log R/G, log B/G)_body − (…)_stripe` is a **constant of the paint pair**, whatever the lamp, the table or the white balance. That is the measurement to make first: for every labelled barrel in the 15 conditions compute (i) the absolute log-chromaticity of body and stripe and (ii) their difference. If (ii) is tight where (i) scatters, the detector should key on (ii). One script, existing labels.
- **References.**
  - Funt & Finlayson, "Color constant color indexing", *PAMI* 17(5):522–529, 1995 — https://doi.org/10.1109/34.391390. **States** (abstract): histograms of ratios of neighbouring RGB values are insensitive to illumination, so no color-constancy preprocessing is needed.
  - Gevers & Smeulders, "Color-based object recognition", *Pattern Recognition* 32(3):453–464, 1999 — https://doi.org/10.1016/S0031-3203(98)00036-3 (PDF https://staff.fnwi.uva.nl/th.gevers/pub/GeversPR99.pdf).
  - Geusebroek, van den Boomgaard, Smeulders, Geerts, "Color invariance", *PAMI* 23(12):1338–1350, 2001 — https://doi.org/10.1109/34.977559.
  - van de Sande, Gevers, Snoek, "Evaluating color descriptors for object and scene recognition", *PAMI* 32(9):1582–1596, 2010 — https://doi.org/10.1109/TPAMI.2009.154 (PDF https://ivi.fnwi.uva.nl/isis/publications/2010/vandeSandeTPAMI2010/vandeSandeTPAMI2010.pdf; table read).
  - Swain & Ballard, "Color indexing", *IJCV* 7(1):11–32, 1991 — https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00130487 (the absolute-color method the above papers repair).

### (c) Illuminant estimation used to move the model, not the pixels; and what auto white balance / auto exposure do to it

- **Idea.** Estimate the light's color from the image by a simple assumption — the scene averages to gray (gray world), the brightest patch is white (white patch / max-RGB), something in between (shades of gray), the average *edge* is gray (gray edge) — then, instead of dividing the image by the estimate, translate the class models by it in log-chromaticity. Under the diagonal model those two are the same arithmetic.
- **A plain statement on the project's rule.** "Shift the model by the estimated light" and "white-balance the pixels, keep the model" give identical answers under the diagonal model. The first respects "never correct the image" to the letter; whether it respects what Dave meant is his call and worth asking before building on it.
- **Needs.** A scene that satisfies the assumption: many differently colored surfaces (gray world), an unclipped white or glossy surface (white patch), many edges between different colors (gray edge).
- **Fails when.** The assumption is false, which on a table top is the normal case: one surface dominates, and the estimate reports the table. On photographs with rich content the published median errors are already several degrees: on the Color-checker set gray world 6.3°, white patch 5.7°, shades of gray 4.0°, gray edge 4.5°; on the Gray-ball set 11.0°, 10.5°, 9.7°, 8.8° (numbers from the survey authors' companion site, see references). This is, I believe, why the project's global-histogram attempts found "nothing predicts the thresholds when a whole place is held out": the statistic measures the place.
- **Auto white balance and auto exposure.**
  - The camera already applies its own gray-world-like gains before the project sees a pixel. Those gains depend on scene content, so a black ottoman or an orange table changes the barrels' recorded color under an unchanged lamp. **Project data:** "the black table under window light shifts the colors as much as the lamp does". Hence the color model cannot be indexed by lamp; it has to be estimated per frame, from something reliable in the frame.
  - Auto exposure sets brightness for the whole scene. On a dark table the barrels are over-exposed: a clipped channel bends hue and drains saturation and nothing downstream can undo it. On a bright table they sink toward the brightness gate.
  - Standard practice is to lock both. **States** (OpenMV camera docs): "You need to turn off white balance too if you want to track colors." and "You need to turn off gain control too if you want to track colors." — https://docs.openmv.io/v4.4.2/library/omv.sensor.html. Cheap sensor modules do expose the switches: the OV2640 datasheet says channel balance "can be done manually by the user or by the internal automatic white balance (AWB) controller" and gain "can either be programmed by the user or controlled by the internal automatic gain control circuit" — https://files.waveshare.com/wiki/common/OV2640DS_en.pdf; the esp32-camera driver has `set_whitebal`, `set_awb_gain`, `set_exposure_ctrl`, `set_gain_ctrl` — https://github.com/espressif/esp32-camera/blob/master/driver/include/sensor.h. (Which sensor the AIM uses is not public.)
  - **On the AIM itself I found no such control.** VEX's websocket library defines commands for driving, screen, sound, LEDs, color / code descriptions and the three detection switches, and nothing for exposure, gain or white balance (`vex/vex_messages.py`, `vex/aim.py`); the API page for AI Vision lists none either. The V5 sensor's SDK does have `vexDeviceAiVisionSensorSet(device, brightness, contrast)` (https://docs.rs/vex-sdk/0.28.0/src/vex_sdk/ai_vision.rs.html), so the firmware family has a knob somewhere. This is the open question already in `STATUS.md`; it is worth one email to VEX, because locked white balance would remove the table-dependent part of the drift at the source.
- **Here.** Do not estimate the light from the whole frame. Estimate it from surfaces whose true color is known: verified barrels (2e), or any part of the robot that is always in view, if there is one (I did not check).

### (d) What RoboCup learned: from look-up tables to self-calibration to learned detectors

- **Where they started is where this project is.** CMVision classifies each pixel by thresholds stored as look-up tables, then builds regions and applies object rules: fixed color classes first, geometry second. It was fast and it was calibrated by hand for every venue.
- **What went wrong, in their words.** Mayer, Utz, Kraetzschmar (2003): "pure color-based image processing is not likely to perform well under varying lighting conditions, even if the vision system is calibrated on a per-game base."
- **What they did about it (2003–2007).** Every successful scheme has the same shape: **use knowledge of what must be where to label pixels without trusting color, then learn the colors from those pixels.**
  - Cameron & Barnes: find landmarks from their known geometry, independent of color; use their pixels to build and keep updating the color table.
  - Jüngel, Hoffmann, Lötzsch; Jüngel: no manual calibration; the color table is built during the game from statistics gathered while recognising objects, with scan-line edge detection carrying the robustness.
  - Heinemann, Sehnke, Streichert, Zell (ACT): the robot's estimated pose plus a model of the field predicts which color class each pixel should show; the table is retrained online from that.
  - Sridharan & Stone: the robot learns its color map from objects at known places, detects a change of illumination by comparing color distributions (KL divergence), and relearns.
  - Anzani, Bosisio, Matteucci, Sorrenti: a parametric color model whose parameters and complexity are adapted online.
  - Not one of these estimates the light from global image statistics. (My reading of the set.)
- **Why most teams ended at learned detectors.** The league removed the color coding and the controlled light. **States** (Röfer, Laue, Richter-Klug, B-Human 2016): "In 2015, the yellow goals were replaced by white ones and in 2016, a black and white ball replaced the previously used orange one. This combination of changes has made simplistic and purely color-based approaches such as 'detect the orange spot' or 'find yellow rectangles' useless."; the outdoor competition "was held in a glass hall and thus under natural lighting conditions". Menashe et al. (2017): "the previous orange ball could be easily distinguished from the surrounding environment using an RGB thresholding approach." Convolutional ball and robot detectors follow from 2016 on (Speck et al.; Cruz et al.; Poppinga & Laue).
- **Here.** The vendor sits at the last stage, this project at the first. But RoboCup left the middle stages because its objects **lost their colors**; the barrels have not. Self-calibration from known structure (2e) and color-difference structure (2h) are still fully available here, and they are the stages this project skipped when it went from fixed thresholds straight to global-statistics adaptation.

### (e) Hypothesize and verify, then re-estimate color from the verified objects

- **Idea.** Propose candidates with a cue that tolerates a cast (a deliberately loose color range, or the color-free stripe pattern of 2h), verify each with rules that do not use absolute color (size expected at that image row, body–stripe–body layout), then re-fit the color model on the pixels of the verified objects and segment again with it. It is expectation-maximisation in plain clothes: guess which pixels are barrel, fit the model to them, repeat once or twice.
- **Needs.** A proposal step with high recall and a verification step strict enough that a false candidate rarely survives; a prior on how far and in which direction the colors may move (2a: one bounded translation in log-chromaticity).
- **Fails when.** A false object passes verification and the model is re-fit to it (the model walks onto the table and confirms itself). No barrel in the frame: nothing to bootstrap from, so the fallback model must keep an empty frame empty. Verification that quietly reuses the same color evidence as the proposal is circular.
- **Here.** One strong safeguard is available for free: every barrel carries **both** colors, so a verified barrel gives an orange sample and a blue sample, and **one** translation must carry both onto their reference positions. A wood table that imitates orange cannot also supply a matching blue. **Project data:** the 2026-10-20 probe found the re-fit "the only thing that helps under casts" (0.44 → 0.57), and the shelved "blue follows the light" rule took the bathroom from 16 to 36 of 60: both are this idea, without the two-color consistency check.
- **References.**

### (f) Histogram backprojection, mean shift, Gaussian / mixture pixel classifiers in chromaticity space with a prior

- **Idea.** Replace the threshold box by a likelihood: how probable is this pixel's chromaticity under "orange barrel", under "blue barrel", under "anything else", combined with a prior by Bayes' rule; the output is a real number per pixel. Backprojection is the look-up-table form (each pixel gets its histogram bin's value); mean shift / CamShift then climbs to the densest spot.
- **Needs.** Labelled pixels for the object models (the project has masks) and, crucially, a **background model**. Swain & Ballard's ratio histogram divides the object histogram by the *current image's* histogram, so a color that is common in this frame (a wood table) is automatically marked down in this frame.
- **Fails when.** The models are still absolute colors, so they move with the light unless shifted (2a, 2c, 2e). The ratio trick turns against you when barrels fill much of the frame (close-ups) or the table truly has the barrel's color. A single Gaussian fits clipped or shadowed pixels badly; mixtures need more data; Jones & Rehg found plain histograms better than mixtures once data is plentiful. Mean shift is a tracker; for one still image it adds nothing.
- **Here.** The project already measured the limit of this family: the best H × S look-up keeps under 90 % of orange on five of six bright wood settings at 1 % background painted. So (f) is an upgrade of *how evidence is expressed* (soft, relative to this frame's background), not a cure for the cast.
- **References.**
  - Swain & Ballard, "Color indexing", *IJCV* 7(1), 1991 (histogram intersection, backprojection with the ratio histogram) — see 2b.
  - Bradski, "Computer vision face tracking for use in a perceptual user interface", *Intel Technology Journal* Q2 1998 (CamShift; no DOI; copy: https://cs400-web.cs.wisc.edu/nchou/p/graphics/Tools/opencv-v1.1/docs/papers/camshift.pdf).
  - Comaniciu, Ramesh, Meer, "Kernel-based object tracking", *PAMI* 25(5):564–577, 2003 — https://doi.org/10.1109/TPAMI.2003.1195991.

  - OpenCV tutorial "Back Projection" — https://docs.opencv.org/4.x/da/d7f/tutorial_back_projection.html (`cv::calcBackProject`; notes that a hue–saturation histogram usually works better than hue alone).

### (g) Hysteresis and region growing from confident seeds

- **Idea.** Use two thresholds: a strict one that only certain barrel pixels pass (seeds), and a loose one that would flood if used alone; keep a loose pixel only if it is connected to a seed. Seeded region growing is the adaptive form: a region absorbs a neighbour while the neighbour resembles the region's own running mean.
- **Needs.** Seeds. A stopping rule (an edge, a size limit).
- **Fails when.** The cast pushes the *whole* barrel outside the strict range: no seeds, nothing grows (the bathroom blue). The loose range connects barrel to a same-colored table: the region leaks.
- **Here.** Useful only in the second half of the pipeline: seeds come from a verified stripe (2h), growth compares each pixel with **that barrel's own body color**, not with a stored range, and stops at a color edge or at the size the geometry allows. That is cast-proof by construction (a comparison within one barrel) and handles two lights in one frame. It also targets a logged failure, "stripe comes out in pieces / body not painted" under highlights.
- **References.** Canny, "A computational approach to edge detection", *PAMI* 8(6):679–698, 1986 — https://doi.org/10.1109/TPAMI.1986.4767851 (hysteresis). Adams & Bischof, "Seeded region growing", *PAMI* 16(6):641–647, 1994 — https://doi.org/10.1109/34.295913. (Citations checked; content from memory.) The Lab 6 page's watershed suggestion is the same family.

### (h) Edge, gradient and structure cues that do not depend on absolute color

- **Idea.** A cast changes what color a surface *is* but hardly changes where colors *change*, in which direction, and in what spatial arrangement. So detect the arrangement: color-opponent edges, the body–stripe–body sandwich as a one-dimensional pattern, an outline template at the size the ground-plane geometry predicts.
- **Four concrete forms.**
  1. *Opponent / ratio edges.* Take a red-against-blue channel such as `log(R/B)` (or `(R−B)/(R+G+B)` to avoid logs of dark pixels) and look at its spatial differences. Under the diagonal model (2a) the light adds a constant to `log(R/B)` everywhere, and a difference removes a constant. Orange against blue is the largest step this channel can show. **Project data:** even where blue has collapsed to gray-olive (hue 26–44, R slightly above B) while orange stays strongly red (S ≈ 200), the step across the stripe edge keeps its sign and most of its size.
  2. *The sandwich as a 1-D matched filter.* Down an image column a barrel reads high–low–high (orange barrel) or low–high–low (blue barrel) in `log(R/B)`, with band heights in a fixed proportion and a total height that the camera geometry gives for that row. Correlate each column with a zero-mean three-band kernel of that height; zero mean cancels the light's constant, normalising by local contrast (normalised cross-correlation) cancels gain too; the sign of the response is the class; add over the columns of the predicted width.
  3. *Outline template at known scale.* Color-gradient edges → distance transform → score a barrel outline of the predicted size at every ground position (chamfer matching). Known scale removes the scale search and most false matches (Hoiem et al.). Lab 6 already points here ("a template matching approach should work well").
  4. *HOG + linear SVM.* Histograms of gradient direction in small cells, contrast-normalised over blocks, fed to a linear classifier. The normalisation is what makes it tolerant of lighting. Note that the weights are *learned*: under the project's rule this belongs in section 3.
- **Needs.** A visible stripe of at least a few pixels (form 1–2); camera height and tilt (have them); gradients computed on color, not gray (orange and blue can have similar brightness).
- **Fails when.** The stripe is hidden or absent: the close-up where only the lid shows (Lab 6 says this needs "a different strategy") and some occlusions. The barrel is far: at f = 400 px a 24 mm barrel is about 32 px tall at 30 cm and 16 px at 60 cm, so the stripe is a handful of pixels, and the camera stream's color resolution may be lower than its brightness resolution (I did not verify the stream's chroma subsampling). Highlights and clipped pixels break the profile; very dark pixels make ratios noisy (the ottoman: 19–27 % of the blue stripe below the brightness gate).
- **Here.** Two properties matter most. (1) **The sandwich survives the orange conflict.** On orange wood the barrel's outer edge disappears, but orange–blue–orange is still there, because the table has no blue band of the right height at the right row. (2) It is a re-ordering of what the detector already does (stripe-anchored, geometry-checked): the stripe is found from color *differences* first, and absolute color is consulted afterwards, if at all.

### (i) Soft evidence instead of hard masks

- **Idea.** Keep a real number per pixel (a log-likelihood ratio, or the opponent value of 2h) and decide once per candidate box, by summing those numbers over the bands the geometry predicts. A pixel slightly off-color then lowers a score slightly instead of vanishing, and a decision rests on hundreds of pixels instead of one.
- **Needs.** Integral images (a running-sum table: any box sum costs four look-ups), which make it cheap to score *every* ground position at its one predicted size. A template built as **inside minus surround** (body bands and stripe band against a ring of table). A threshold on the summed score, chosen on train frames. A rule for overlapping candidates (the step where the vendor produces its duplicates; known barrel footprints on the ground make it cleaner here).
- **Fails when.** Sums are taken without a surround: a large area of mildly barrel-like color (wood) outscores a small barrel. Occlusion lowers the score of a real barrel: score parts separately. One threshold may not suit every condition; contrast-normalised scores help.
- **Here.** This is the smallest change to the present design that removes its sharpest edge (the hard gate before any shape is seen). Before neural detectors this recipe, soft channels + integral images, was the state of the art.
- **References.**
  - Viola & Jones, "Rapid object detection using a boosted cascade of simple features", CVPR 2001 — https://doi.org/10.1109/CVPR.2001.990517 (the integral image).
  - Dollár, Tu, Perona, Belongie, "Integral channel features", BMVC 2009 — https://doi.org/10.5244/C.23.91. **States** (full text): ten channels (LUV color, gradient magnitude, six gradient-direction bins); all ten at 40 fps on 320 × 240; training "takes about 5-10 minutes".

## 3. Lightweight learned options (if the "no learned detectors" rule is revisited)

### What about 300 frames from about 15 conditions can and cannot support

- **The number that matters is 15, not 300.** Frames within one condition share the table, the lamp and the camera's exposure state; they are near-copies for the question "does it work in a new place". For that question there are about 15 examples.
- **Can support:** fitting models with tens to a few hundred free numbers (a Gaussian per class, a small look-up table with smoothing, a linear verifier); fine-tuning a pretrained detector to good results *in those conditions and ones like them*; a leave-one-condition-out score (15 noisy numbers).
- **Cannot support:** a claim of robustness to unseen light with any statistical weight; training a detector from scratch; much hyper-parameter search (each choice made by looking at held-out conditions leaks them; pre-registration matters more here, not less).
- **The test set cannot settle close calls.** 95 % intervals (Wilson) on the 2026-11-25 look: vendor 23 of 32 = 72 % (55–84 %); ours 14 of 32 = 44 % (28–61 %). The gap is probably real; a gap of ten points would not be measurable on 32 barrels.

### Option A: a learned per-pixel classifier (look-up table, random forest, small network)

- **What.** Keep the whole detector; replace only the hue / saturation box by a classifier that outputs "orange / blue / neither" per pixel, from the pixel's chromaticity **plus context**: its value minus this frame's median below the horizon, minus the mean of a ring around it, its image row, its brightness. A forest over such pixel-difference features is the "semantic texton forest" idea; a learned 3-D table is what RoboCup's CMVision used.
- **Data.** Millions of labelled pixels exist already; they are heavily correlated, so the real limit is again the 15 conditions.
- **Pitfalls.** Splitting train / test by pixel or by frame instead of by condition (gives flattering nonsense). Learning the training tables ("wood-colored means not barrel") and failing on the sixteenth table. Label noise at barrel edges; barrels are under 2 % of pixels.
- **Illumination.** Relative features, plus an augmentation that is exact under the diagonal model and costs nothing: add a random bounded translation in log-chromaticity to all features of a training frame.
- **Verdict.** The smallest step and still explainable (a table or a few shallow trees can be drawn). But the project's ceiling check already bounds what color-only evidence can do on wood, so the gain must come from the context features.
- **References.** Shotton, Johnson, Cipolla, "Semantic texton forests for image categorization and segmentation", CVPR 2008 — https://doi.org/10.1109/CVPR.2008.4587503 (random forests on pixel differences). Shotton, Winn, Rother, Criminisi, "TextonBoost", ECCV 2006 — https://doi.org/10.1007/11744023_1 (boosted texture-layout filters, not forests). Content of both from memory; citations checked.

### Option B: window classifiers on gradient / channel features (HOG + linear SVM, ACF, Viola–Jones cascades)

- **What.** Slide a window of the geometry-predicted size, describe it by contrast-normalised gradient histograms (HOG) or summed channels (ACF), classify with a linear SVM or boosted shallow trees.
- **Data.** For a rigid object seen from one height, hundreds of positives are enough: the far harder INRIA pedestrian task was trained on 1237 cropped pedestrians and 1218 person-free images (**states:** Dollár et al. 2014). The project has several hundred barrel instances, doubled by mirroring.
- **Pitfalls.** Hard negatives come from only 15 backgrounds, so the detector learns those backgrounds. ACF's color channels are absolute (LUV): they would re-import the cast problem unless replaced by ratio channels or trained with cast augmentation. Tooling is dated (OpenCV's cascade trainer is legacy; the reference ACF code is MATLAB) — this last point is from memory.
- **Illumination.** Block normalisation handles brightness and contrast well; color casts only matter through the color channels.
- **Verdict.** Most attractive as a **verifier** on candidates the classical stage proposes (a linear weight image that can be shown and explained), not as a replacement.
- **References.** Dalal & Triggs 2005; Viola & Jones 2001; Dollár et al. 2009, 2014 (all above).

### Option C: a tiny CNN detector, fine-tuned (YOLO "nano" class)

- **What.** Take a detector pretrained on a large public set and fine-tune it on the barrel frames.
- **Size and speed** (**states:** Ultralytics model tables, 640 px input, CPU not named): YOLOv8n 3.2 M parameters, 8.7 GFLOPs, 80 ms per image on CPU; YOLO11n 2.6 M, 6.5, 56 ms; YOLO26n 2.4 M, 5.5, 39 ms — https://docs.ultralytics.com/models/yolov8/ · https://docs.ultralytics.com/models/yolo11/ · https://docs.ultralytics.com/models/yolo26/. Fine on a laptop. For the record, such models have run on robot hardware: xYOLO at 9.66 fps on a Raspberry Pi 3 B (Barry, Shah, Keijsers, Khan, Hopman, IVCNZ 2019 — https://doi.org/10.1109/IVCNZ48456.2019.8960963); ROBO (Szemenyei & Estivill-Castro, RoboCup 2019 — https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-35699-6_24).
- **Data.** Vendor guidance, not evidence: Ultralytics recommends "≥ 1500 images per class" and "≥ 10000 instances (labeled objects) per class", plus "about 0-10% background images to help reduce FPs" — https://docs.ultralytics.com/yolov5/tutorials/tips_for_best_training_results/. Roboflow suggests "collecting 50-100 images" for a first model — https://roboflow.com/blog/how-much-training-data. (Both quotes came through a summarising fetch; check the pages before quoting them onward.) The project sits at about a fifth of the first figure in images and a tenth or less in instances. Published few-shot results show fine-tuning the last layers is a strong baseline (Wang et al., "Frustratingly simple few-shot object detection", ICML 2020 — https://proceedings.mlr.press/v119/wang20j.html) but do not show that a few hundred images give a robust detector; I found no rigorous learning curve for one or two simple classes.
- **Stretching the data.** Cut-and-paste synthesis fits this project unusually well, because barrel masks exist and barrel-free photos of new tables need no labelling: Dwibedi, Misra, Hebert, "Cut, paste and learn", ICCV 2017 — https://doi.org/10.1109/ICCV.2017.146 (**states**, abstract: over 21 % relative gain when added to real images; with 10 % of the real data it beats all-real training in a cross-domain test). Related: Tobin et al., "Domain randomization", IROS 2017 — https://doi.org/10.1109/IROS.2017.8202133.
- **Illumination — the part people get wrong.**
  - A CNN is **not** cast-proof by nature. Afifi & Brown, "What else can fool deep learning? Addressing color constancy errors on deep neural network performance", ICCV 2019 — https://doi.org/10.1109/ICCV.2019.00033. **States:** white-balance errors cost six ImageNet classifiers about 10 points of top-1 accuracy; generic hue / saturation jitter helped partly and sometimes hurt clean accuracy, and "does not well represent the color casts produced by incorrect WB"; white-balance-specific augmentation helped more. (Classification and segmentation, not detection.)
  - The default jitter is too small for the measured drift. Ultralytics defaults: `hsv_h` 0.015, `hsv_s` 0.7, `hsv_v` 0.4 — https://docs.ultralytics.com/usage/cfg/. A hue jitter of 1.5 % of the circle is about ±3 OpenCV hue units (my conversion); the ottoman moves orange by 7–9 and the bathroom moves blue by far more.
  - Here **color is the class label**, so augmentation must not turn orange into blue: use bounded per-channel gains (casts along realistic lights), not free hue rotation. Zini et al., "Planckian jitter", ICLR 2023 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07993: standard color jitter weakens the color information in learned features; physically realistic illuminant jitter keeps it (self-supervised classification, not detection).
  - I found **no** source showing that a detector trained with color jitter keeps working under real casts; it is common practice, not a demonstrated guarantee.
- **Pitfalls.** Close-ups and occlusions must be in the training set or it will share the vendor's blind spots. Pasted composites lack shadows and reflections (the glass table). Failures cannot be read off the way "hue 16 is outside 3–13" can. And Dave's standing rule that Tanush can explain the main program is hard to meet with a network.
- **Verdict.** Very likely good on the 15 conditions and their neighbours within a week of work; behaviour on a new table or lamp is no more predictable from 15 conditions than the hand detector's.

### Option D (my suggestion): classical proposals + a small learned verifier

Keep stripe finding and geometry as the proposer; learn only "is this normalised crop a barrel" with a linear model on contrast-normalised features. Lowest data need, weights can be displayed, recall is capped by the proposer (so it removes false boxes, it does not find missed barrels). **Inference**, no single source.

## 4. Ranked recommendation

### If the detector must stay classical: the three ideas with the most robustness to a color cast per unit of complexity

**First, one measurement before any detector code** (existing labels, one script, pre-registrable): for every labelled barrel in the 15 conditions, the log-chromaticity of body and stripe, and their difference. Prediction from 2a / 2b: the absolute values scatter across conditions; the body-minus-stripe difference stays tight; orange, blue and each table move in parallel. If the prediction holds, ideas 1 and 2 are justified by the project's own data; if it fails (the camera's color processing is too far from diagonal), that is learned for the price of a script.

1. **Find the stripe from color differences, not from color** (2h forms 1–2, scored softly as in 2i; theory 2a, 2b). A zero-mean body–stripe–body kernel on a red-against-blue log-ratio channel, at the height the geometry predicts for each row, summed over the predicted width.
   *Why first:* it needs no estimate of the light at all, so there is nothing to estimate wrongly; the cast, the camera's white balance and the table's color all cancel in the difference; it is the one cue that survives the "orange conflict" (ottoman orange at hue 16–18 against a bathroom floor at 16–24), because a floor has no blue band of the right height at the right row; and it is a re-ordering of the stripe-anchored detector that already exists, not a new detector. It is also, in hand-written form, the kind of evidence the vendor's network uses.
   *Limits:* needs a visible stripe a few pixels tall; the lid-only close-up keeps its separate path; very dark and clipped pixels must be excluded.
2. **Re-estimate absolute color from the barrels just verified, as one bounded translation in log-chromaticity that must fit orange and blue together** (2e, with 2a and 2c). Then re-segment with the shifted model to get body extents and to pick up barrels whose stripe was hidden.
   *Why second:* two numbers per frame instead of six thresholds; the estimate comes from surfaces of known color rather than from the table; the two-color agreement is a built-in guard against bootstrapping from a false object; with no verified barrel the stored model applies unchanged, so empty frames and today's protected conditions behave as now.
   *Limits:* depends on idea 1 finding at least one barrel; mathematically equivalent to white-balancing (ask Dave, 2c).
3. **Grow each body outward from its verified stripe by similarity to that barrel's own color, stopping at a color edge or at the predicted size** (2g).
   *Why third:* box extents without any stored color; indifferent to mixed light; aimed at logged failures ("stripe in pieces", "body not painted", highlights). Cheap to add once idea 1 supplies seeds.
   *Limits:* leaks where table and body are the same color, so the geometric size limit must be the final word there.

Deliberately not in the three: illuminant estimators on the whole frame (they measure the table, 2c); the one-dimensional illuminant-invariant image (it discards the orange–blue axis, 2a); mean shift (a tracker); mixture models fit by EM without structure (drift onto the table, 2e/2f). The best fix of all, locking the camera's white balance and exposure, is not available through any public AIM interface I could find: one email to VEX.

### Switching to a small learned model: the single strongest argument each way

- **For.** The gap to the vendor (23 against 14 of 32) lies entirely on the axis where learned detectors earn their keep, appearance varying with light and surface, and the vendor *is* one. The project now owns what it lacked in October: about 300 labelled frames with masks, a geometry model, and therefore the means to synthesise thousands of composites on new tables at no labelling cost. A fine-tuned nano detector, or more conservatively a learned verifier behind the classical proposer (option D), is the shortest path to vendor-level recall, and the hand-built geometry would keep the project's existing advantages (close range, occlusion, fewer false boxes).
- **Against.** It would rebuild the vendor's approach with a small fraction of the vendor's data, and 15 conditions cannot show whether the result generalises: the project would trade a detector whose every miss can be explained and fixed ("orange read hue 16, the sector ends at 13") for one whose behaviour on the sixteenth table can be neither predicted nor explained, and whose claimed gain cannot be resolved on 32 test barrels. The classical route has not been exhausted: the cast-proof cues of section 2 (differences, structure, bootstrapping) have not been tried yet, and they are the part of the vendor's advantage that can be written by hand.
